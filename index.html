Six months of transaction history behind every alert. The signal is real, but it isn't in any single number.

WIUT Hackathon 2026 · FinTech / AI in Finance. This page documents how we explored the alert-escalation dataset, what we found in the transaction histories, and how those findings shaped our modeling approach.

1. Our approach

Each alert (signal_id) comes with roughly 180 days of raw transaction history. We treated this as a table-of-tables problem: summarize each alert's transaction history into a fixed-width feature vector, then train a gradient-boosted classifier to output a probability of escalation, evaluated by ROC-AUC.

We worked in three stages: (1) verify the data has no leakage and understand its structure, (2) run EDA to find out where — if anywhere — escalated and dismissed alerts actually differ, (3) let those findings drive feature engineering rather than throwing every conceivable statistic at the model. We validated every claim against the data itself rather than assuming column meanings.

2. Dataset overview & structure

Five files: two per split (signals, transactions) plus a submission template. Transactions are historical and link to alerts many-to-one.

FileRowsGrain
train_signals.csv14,0001 row / alert, includes target
test_signals.csv6,0001 row / alert, no target
train_transactions.parquet~6.99M1 row / historical transaction
test_transactions.parquet~3.03M1 row / historical transaction

Transaction volume per alert is highly variable — median around 460 transactions, ranging from 1 to roughly 2,300. Two categorical fields describe each transaction: direction (kirim incoming / chiqim outgoing) and type (karta bank_otkazmasi naqd xalqaro), plus a standardized size indicator (not raw currency). No missing values, no duplicate IDs, and zero signal_id overlap between train and test.

Leakage check: only 0.012% of transaction rows fell after their alert's date, and every one of those was a same-day timestamp artifact (0-day gap) — not genuine future information. History windows are fixed at almost exactly 180 days for 99% of alerts, which looks like a generation artifact rather than organic account age, so we excluded it as a feature.

3. EDA: target & time

Class balance: 11,595 dismissed vs. 2,405 escalated. Not extreme — ROC-AUC handles this fine.
Monthly escalation rate stays in a 14%–21% band with no trend — supports a random stratified split rather than a time-based one.

4. Key observations from transaction history

  1. No single dominant feature. Every simple aggregate we tried (mean/std/max transaction size, direction share) correlated with the target at |r| ≤ 0.06 — the escalated and dismissed distributions visibly overlap.
  2. Two AML-style hypotheses tested, both weak alone. "Recent activity vs. the alert's own 180-day baseline" and "international outgoing transfers" are classic red flags — both showed the expected direction but only a small effect.
  3. Fixed 180-day history window across 99% of alerts, confirming this is a generation-time design choice, not customer tenure.
  4. The signal is interaction-based, not linear. A tree model beat logistic regression by a wide, consistent margin — evidence the useful information lives in combinations of features, not in any one of them (see §5 effect sizes below).

5. Target distribution & behavioral patterns

Standardized mean difference (Cohen's d) between escalated and dismissed alerts, for the features that mattered most. All effects are small individually, which is itself the finding.

Escalated alerts skew toward slightly lower peak transaction size and net outflow, with more cash (naqd) activity and more active days — but every bar sits under |d| = 0.16.

Because no individual signal is strong, we ran a baseline gradient-boosted model purely to test whether real, extractable signal exists at all despite the weak correlations:

6. Feature engineering motivated by EDA

Each addition below was a direct response to an EDA finding, not a generic aggregate sweep.

Feature groupMotivated by
Distribution shape (quantiles, skew, kurtosis)Weak means/std, but shape differences were the top model-importance features
Per-category 30-day recency + drift from own baselineThe "deviation from own baseline" hypothesis — turned out to be the single strongest new group
Weekly transaction-count / amount trend slopeTesting whether a ramp-up or quiet-down right before the alert matters, beyond a single before/after split
Explicit interactions (net flow, type ratios)Confirming the model was combining features, not just finding one we'd missed
Honest result: adding all 41 new features naively lowered CV AUC slightly (0.598 vs. 0.600) — noise diluted the signal. Selecting the top 25 features by importance recovered and then improved on the baseline, reaching 0.606 mean CV AUC, confirmed stable across an independent CV split (0.607) and consistent across both CatBoost and LightGBM. The lesson: more features only through the lens of what the model can actually use, not a raw feature count.

7. Conclusion

This is a genuinely hard, low-but-real-signal dataset — consistent with data that has been deliberately synthesized and localized. There is no shortcut feature: escalation is driven by combinations of distribution shape, deviation from an alert's own historical baseline, and category-level activity drift, which only tree-based models could extract. We validated this with a stratified 5-fold CV harness reused identically across five model families, and improved our best CV AUC from 0.600 → 0.606 through EDA-motivated feature selection rather than blind feature accumulation. We see realistic further headroom in the 0.60–0.68 range with hyperparameter tuning and further-refined feature selection, and we are reporting these numbers as measured rather than overstating them.
